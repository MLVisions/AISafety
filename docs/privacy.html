Threats & Misinformation

Recent threat reporting shows AI is no longer just lowering the cost of scams, it is increasing their speed, realism, and scale. The FBI warned in December 2024 that criminals are using generative AI to create highly convincing text, images, audio, and video for fraud and social engineering (FBI, 2024, 2024-12-03). In early 2025, Europol reported that criminal networks are already using AI for phishing, impersonation, document forgery, and multilingual fraud operations, and that these tools reduce technical barriers for organized crime according to Europol's 2025 Serious and Organised Crime Threat Assessment (Europol, 2025, 2025-03-18).

Voice-cloning and deepfake scams have moved from novelty to routine fraud infrastructure. The FTC warned consumers in March 2024 that scammers are using AI voice cloning in family-emergency and impersonation scams (FTC, 2024, 2024-03-20). In one widely cited corporate case, a finance worker in Hong Kong was tricked into transferring about HK$200 million after fraudsters used AI-generated deepfake video and audio in a fake video conference (Hong Kong Police Force, 2024, 2024-02-04). Security researchers also found that large language models can make phishing and business email compromise more persuasive by improving grammar, tone-matching, and localization, which lowers the traditional signals people used to detect scams as documented by the UK's National Cyber Security Centre guidance on AI and cyber threat (NCSC, 2024, 2024-01-24).

Agentic fraud is the next step. Instead of a human scammer manually sending messages, AI agents can now draft messages, scrape public data, adapt their scripts, and respond in real time across email, SMS, voice, and chat channels. The U.S. Treasury's FinCEN warned in 2024 that criminals are using generative AI tools to facilitate fraud schemes, including synthetic identity fraud, document generation, and more convincing social engineering (FinCEN, 2024, 2024-11-07). OpenAI separately reported disrupting malicious campaigns in 2024 and 2025 that used AI tools for social engineering, phishing content, and scam enablement (OpenAI, 2024, 2024-06-05) and additional covert influence and cyber-related abuse activity (OpenAI, 2025, 2025-03-21). The practical risk for individuals is that scams can become continuous, personalized, and available 24 hours a day.

AI-powered phishing and credential harvesting are becoming harder to spot because the content can be customized at scale. Google's Threat Intelligence Group assessed in January 2025 that generative AI is already helping threat actors with reconnaissance, scripting, translation, and phishing content generation, even if it has not yet transformed top-tier intrusion capability (Google Threat Intelligence Group, 2025, 2025-01-29). The UK's NCSC similarly concluded that AI will almost certainly increase the volume and impact of cyber intrusions by enabling more effective phishing and social engineering by 2025 (NCSC, 2024, 2024-01-24). That means you should treat polished grammar, natural conversation, and even familiar voices as weak signals of legitimacy.

Surveillance technology is also expanding. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission took action in 2024 against data broker Mobilewalla for allegedly collecting and selling granular location data tied to sensitive characteristics and audience segments (FTC, 2024, 2024-12-03). Separately, the U.S. Government Accountability Office found in September 2024 that federal agencies have increasingly used personal data obtained from data brokers, creating oversight and privacy concerns (GAO, 2024, 2024-09-05). In public spaces, facial recognition and video analytics remain contested because they can identify, track, and categorize people at scale; the UK's Information Commissioner's Office has continued publishing guidance that such systems must meet strict data protection requirements under UK law (ICO, 2025, accessed 2026-03-25).

Predictive profiling and behavioral manipulation are a quieter but important threat. The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warned in 2024 that firms can use digital surveillance, algorithmic scoring, and behavioral targeting in ways that shape consumer choices and may exploit vulnerabilities (CFPB, 2024, 2024-01-10). Academic research has also shown that machine learning models can infer sensitive attributes, including political orientation, mental health signals, and demographic traits, from seemingly ordinary digital traces as summarized by the National Academies report on emerging technologies and privacy (National Academies, 2023, 2023-01-12). When these inferences are used for pricing, advertising, hiring, or political messaging, people may be nudged or filtered without realizing how much has been inferred about them.

For individuals, the practical takeaway is simple. Voice, face, writing style, and urgency are no longer reliable proof of identity. Stronger defenses now include using phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication such as hardware security keys or passkeys (CISA, 2024, 2024-10-23), verifying money requests through a separate trusted channel, limiting public exposure of voice samples and personal details, and reviewing app permissions and data broker opt-out options where available. These steps will not eliminate AI-enabled threats, but they can reduce the chance that automated scams and profiling systems succeed.

Security Best Practices

Individuals and organizations should update security practices for a threat environment shaped by AI-assisted phishing, voice cloning, synthetic video, and automated social engineering. Current guidance increasingly prioritizes phishing-resistant authentication, independent identity verification, privacy-preserving communications, and rehearsed incident response.

  • Use phishing-resistant authentication first. CISA recommends phishing-resistant MFA, such as FIDO/WebAuthn authenticators (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25), and NIST SP 800-63B states that passkeys are phishing-resistant authenticators (NIST, draft accessed 2026-03-25). For high-risk accounts, Google advises enrolling security keys or passkeys for stronger protection than older second factors (Google, accessed 2026-03-25). This matters because AI-generated phishing pages and real-time social engineering make SMS codes and app prompts easier to steal or trick users into approving.
  • Treat biometrics as convenient, not sufficient on their own. NIST warns that biometrics are not secrets and must be bound to a physical authenticator or used with another factor (NIST, draft accessed 2026-03-25). The FBI warned in 2024 that criminals are using AI-generated voice messages in fraud schemes (FBI IC3, 2024-04-25), which makes voice verification alone increasingly risky. Face and voice checks can still help with device unlock or liveness checks, but they should not be the only gate for payments, account recovery, or executive approvals.
  • Verify identity out of band before acting on urgent requests. CISA advises users to verify unusual or urgent messages through a separate trusted channel (CISA, 2023-10-18). In practice, that means calling a known number, using a pre-shared family or workplace code word, or confirming in person before sending money, credentials, or sensitive files. This is especially important when a message includes emotional pressure, secrecy, or a request to bypass normal process.
  • Use deepfake detection and provenance tools, but do not rely on them alone. NIST's Face Analysis Technology Evaluation found that face analysis technologies can be vulnerable to presentation attacks and vary significantly by method and conditions (NIST, accessed 2026-03-25). For media authenticity, the C2PA open standard allows publishers and tools to attach provenance metadata showing how content was created or edited (C2PA, 2025-11-18 specification), and Adobe's Content Credentials system implements this approach for images and other media (Adobe, accessed 2026-03-25). Provenance metadata can help when present, but absence of metadata is not proof of fakery, so high-stakes decisions still require independent verification.
  • Prefer encrypted, privacy-preserving communication tools. Signal documents that its messaging protocol provides end-to-end encryption so service providers cannot read message contents (Signal, accessed 2026-03-25), and WhatsApp states that personal messages and calls are protected with end-to-end encryption by default (WhatsApp, accessed 2026-03-25). For network privacy, CISA recommends using a VPN on public Wi-Fi to help protect internet traffic from interception (CISA, 2022-08-18). These tools do not stop every scam, but they reduce exposure to interception, account takeover, and unnecessary data collection.
  • Harden routine account security for the current threat landscape. CISA recommends using long, unique passwords stored in a password manager (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25), turning on automatic software updates (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25), and recognizing and reporting phishing (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25). Organizations should also limit admin privileges, separate approval chains for payments or wire changes, and log unusual sign-in behavior. AI lowers the cost of producing convincing lures, so small process controls now matter more.
  • Prepare an incident response path before something goes wrong. The FTC advises victims of scams or identity theft to report incidents at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IdentityTheft.gov (FTC, accessed 2026-03-25), while the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center accepts reports of cyber-enabled fraud and online crime (FBI IC3, accessed 2026-03-25). For organizations, CISA's incident response guidance recommends preserving logs, containing affected systems, resetting compromised credentials, and notifying appropriate internal and external stakeholders (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25). A practical plan should specify who confirms suspected deepfakes, who freezes payments, how employees escalate suspicious requests, and when legal, regulatory, or law-enforcement reporting is required.

A simple rule of thumb is to trust process over media. A familiar voice, face, or writing style is no longer enough to prove identity. The safer approach is to combine phishing-resistant authentication (NIST, draft accessed 2026-03-25), out-of-band verification (CISA, 2023-10-18), and encrypted communication (Signal, accessed 2026-03-25) for any action involving money, credentials, sensitive data, or public communications.

Data Privacy & Ethics

In 2025-2026, privacy and AI governance are being shaped by a mix of enforcement, new legislation, and operational guidance. In Europe, the EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with prohibited AI practices applying from 2 February 2025, general-purpose AI obligations beginning 2 August 2025, and most remaining rules applying from 2 August 2026 (EU, 2024). Alongside this, the European Data Protection Board adopted Opinion 28/2024 on data protection and AI models on 17 December 2024 (EDPB, 2024), clarifying that AI developers and deployers must assess lawful basis, transparency, and whether models can retain personal data. For readers and organizations, the practical takeaway is to assume that training, fine-tuning, and model deployment can all trigger privacy obligations, not only downstream app use.

Privacy enforcement remains active under the GDPR. The Irish Data Protection Commission fined LinkedIn Ireland EUR 310 million on 24 October 2024 for GDPR infringements related to behavioural analysis and targeted advertising legal bases (Irish DPC, 2024). The Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Uber EUR 290 million on 26 August 2024 over transfers of European driver data to the United States without appropriate safeguards (Dutch DPA, 2024). These cases reinforce a core lesson: organizations need a documented lawful basis, strict purpose limitation, and valid transfer mechanisms, especially when AI systems centralize user, worker, or customer data across borders.

In the United States, the privacy landscape is still fragmented, but state coverage continues to expand. The International Association of Privacy Professionals reported on 16 January 2025 that eight state privacy laws took effect in 2025, adding to five already in force, and that six more are scheduled to take effect in 2026 (IAPP, 2025). At the federal level, comprehensive privacy legislation remains unsettled. The American Privacy Rights Act discussion draft was released by the US Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation and the House Committee on Energy and Commerce on 7 April 2024 (US Senate Commerce Committee, 2024), but Congress.gov shows no enacted comprehensive federal privacy law as of 25 March 2026 (US Congress, accessed 2026-03-25). For compliance planning, this means companies should continue to build around state-law requirements such as consumer access, deletion, correction, opt-out rights, and data-protection assessments.

AI governance is also moving toward auditability and accountability. In the EU, the AI Act requires providers of high-risk AI systems to establish risk-management systems, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, and post-market monitoring (EU, 2024). In the United States, the National Institute of Standards and Technology released the AI Risk Management Framework on 26 January 2023 and a Generative AI Profile on 26 July 2024 (NIST, 2023-2024), giving organizations a practical structure for governance, testing, red-teaming, and documentation even where no single federal AI law exists. In Canada, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada published principles for responsible, trustworthy, and privacy-protective generative AI technologies on 7 December 2023 (OPC Canada, 2023), and these remain a useful benchmark while broader AI legislation is still evolving.

Consent and data minimization are becoming more important as organizations deploy AI features into ordinary products. The UK Information Commissioner's Office updated its guidance on anonymisation and privacy-enhancing technologies on 28 March 2024 (ICO, 2024), emphasizing that anonymisation must be assessed realistically, not assumed. The European Data Protection Board's Opinion 28/2024 states that whether AI model training can rely on legitimate interests depends on a case-specific balancing test and effective safeguards (EDPB, 2024). In practice, best practice is to collect only the fields needed for a defined use case, avoid feeding sensitive or confidential data into public models unless contractually and technically protected, maintain short retention periods, and give users meaningful controls over training, personalization, and data sharing.

Corporate accountability expectations are rising as regulators ask for evidence, not broad ethical statements. OpenAI published an updated Safety Evaluations Hub on 14 May 2025 (OpenAI, 2025), while Google described its Frontier Safety Framework on 25 July 2024 (Google DeepMind, 2024) and Anthropic detailed its Responsible Scaling Policy updates on 5 March 2025 (Anthropic, 2025). These disclosures do not replace legal compliance, but they show a broader shift toward publishing testing thresholds, escalation procedures, and governance commitments. For organizations adopting AI, the more durable standard is to pair public principles with internal controls, board or senior-management oversight, audit trails, incident response plans, and documented vendor due diligence.

Cross-border data transfers remain a live issue. The European Commission adopted the EU-US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision on 10 July 2023 (European Commission, 2023), and the Commission's first review, published 9 October 2024, concluded that US authorities and participating companies had put the framework's key elements in place (European Commission, 2024). The United Kingdom also advanced its own transfer architecture, with the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 receiving Royal Assent on 19 June 2025 (UK Parliament, 2025) and the UK government publishing guidance on the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (UK Government, 2025). In Australia, the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024 (Australian Government, 2024), strengthening enforcement and introducing new privacy protections. The practical implication is that international data transfers are still possible, but they increasingly require transfer impact assessments, contract review, localization analysis where relevant, and continuous monitoring of adequacy and enforcement changes.
