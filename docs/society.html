Society & Mental Health

Navigating labour disruption, identity and wellbeing

Mental Health & Labour Disruption

Recent research suggests that AI-related labour disruption is better understood as a wave of job reallocation, task redesign, and uneven sectoral shock, rather than a single number for jobs "lost." The World Economic Forum estimates that 92 million jobs will be displaced by 2030, while 170 million new roles may be created, for a net gain of 78 million jobs (WEF, 2025). This is a notable evolution from the WEF's earlier estimate that 83 million jobs could be displaced and 69 million created by 2027 (WEF, 2023). Goldman Sachs' widely cited exposure estimate remains that generative AI could automate work equivalent to 300 million full-time jobs globally (Goldman Sachs, 2023), but that figure refers to potential task exposure, not a forecast that 300 million people will become unemployed. OECD analysis has moved in the same direction, stressing that jobs are rarely fully automated, while many occupations face substantial task-level change, especially in high-exposure knowledge work (OECD, 2024) and that generative AI exposure is often highest in jobs requiring tertiary education because language-intensive tasks are easier to automate than many manual ones (OECD, 2023).

The sectors under the greatest immediate pressure are those with routine cognitive tasks, standardized workflows, and heavy use of text, images, scheduling, or documentation. The WEF reports that clerical and secretarial roles, including cashiers, ticket clerks, administrative assistants, and executive secretaries, are among the fastest-declining occupations through 2030 (WEF, 2025). OECD evidence similarly finds that finance, insurance, information services, professional services, and public administration contain many occupations highly exposed to generative AI (OECD, 2023). At the firm level, early disruption has been visible in customer support, translation, basic content production, and some junior software and back-office functions, while industrial robotics continues to affect warehousing and manufacturing, where the International Federation of Robotics recorded 4.28 million industrial robots operating in factories worldwide in 2023, up 10% from the prior year (IFR, 2024).

Mental health risks rise when workers face prolonged uncertainty, not only when layoffs occur. A broad evidence base shows that unemployment is associated with significantly higher risks of depression, anxiety, psychological distress, and lower life satisfaction (Norström et al., 2021). Job insecurity also matters independently: a systematic review found that perceived job insecurity is consistently associated with poorer mental health, including depressive symptoms and emotional exhaustion (Kim and von dem Knesebeck, 2020). This is especially relevant in the AI era because workers may remain employed while experiencing chronic fear that their role is being hollowed out, downgraded, or made temporary.

Emerging AI-specific research suggests that technology fears can intensify these pressures. The OECD found in a cross-country employer-employee study that workers in highly digitalized workplaces often reported positive effects from AI on dangerous or tedious tasks, but concerns about surveillance, loss of autonomy, and job security remained significant (OECD, 2023). Separately, the American Psychological Association reports that work-related stress remains strongly tied to economic insecurity, low control, and uncertainty about the future (APA, 2024). For many workers, AI disruption is therefore experienced less as a single event and more as a sustained identity threat, especially in occupations that were once seen as stable pathways into the middle class.

Identity disruption is an important, and often overlooked, part of labour-market shock. Psychological and sociological research has long shown that work provides routine, status, social connection, and meaning; when employment becomes unstable, people can experience shame, social withdrawal, and a fractured sense of self. A major review in The Lancet concluded that unemployment and poor-quality employment are both associated with worse mental health, while secure and supportive work is generally protective (The Lancet Public Health, 2020). In practice, this means AI-related disruption can harm wellbeing even before income falls sharply, because anticipation, status loss, and uncertainty can be psychologically destabilizing.

Vulnerability is uneven across demographic groups. The OECD reports that workers with higher education are often more exposed to generative AI at the task level, but workers with lower education remain more vulnerable to displacement from earlier forms of automation and may have fewer resources for retraining (OECD, 2023). The WEF finds that skills instability is highest in roles facing rapid technological change, and access to reskilling remains unequal across income levels, firms, and countries (WEF, 2025). Geography matters too: local economies that depend heavily on logistics, routine office work, call centres, or a narrow set of employers face greater risk of concentrated distress when automation arrives faster than replacement jobs. Younger workers can be exposed because entry-level pathways are being automated, while older workers can face longer unemployment spells and steeper retraining barriers if displaced.

Recent case evidence shows how these pressures can accumulate in real communities. In the United States, the shift toward automated fulfilment and logistics has brought productivity gains, but researchers at the UC Berkeley Labor Center note that warehouse jobs are increasingly shaped by algorithmic management, intense monitoring, and injury risk, with consequences for stress and worker wellbeing (UC Berkeley Labor Center, 2024). In the United Kingdom, public reporting on AI adoption in customer-service and administrative functions has been concentrated in regions where back-office employment is a major source of stable work, heightening local concern about white-collar displacement as firms test generative AI to reduce routine support tasks (Bank of England, 2024). These cases do not prove mass unemployment, but they do show how even partial automation can reshape local opportunity structures and mental health risk.

For individuals, the practical takeaway is to prepare for task change before job loss occurs. Workers in exposed roles can reduce risk by auditing which parts of their job are routine and document-based, building complementary skills such as client communication, judgment, compliance, supervision, and domain expertise, and seeking training before a crisis forces a rushed transition. Mental health preparation matters too: evidence consistently shows that social support, early counselling, financial planning, and active job-search or retraining routines can buffer the psychological harm associated with unemployment and insecurity by reducing isolation and restoring a sense of control (APA, 2024). The core lesson from the latest research is balanced but clear: AI is unlikely to eliminate work wholesale in the near term, but it is already changing who feels secure, which communities are exposed, and how economic uncertainty translates into anxiety, depression, and identity strain.

Economic Uncertainty & Resilience

Recent evidence suggests that AI will change work unevenly rather than eliminate all work at once. The IMF estimated that about 40% of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI, with advanced economies facing higher exposure but also greater potential productivity gains (IMF, 2024-01-14). The OECD reported that workers in more AI-exposed occupations are not yet seeing broad employment collapse, but they are experiencing meaningful task change and pressure to adapt skills (OECD Employment Outlook, 2024-07). For individuals, that means resilience depends less on predicting one dramatic shock and more on preparing for repeated transitions, shorter skill cycles, and localized disruption.

Government policy matters because workers adapt more successfully when training is paired with income support, career guidance, and accessible services. In the United States, the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act remains the main federal framework for job training and career services delivered through American Job Centers (U.S. Department of Labor, accessed 2026-03-25). The United States has also expanded direct support for displaced workers through Trade Adjustment Assistance alternatives and unemployment insurance modernization efforts, while many states now fund short-term credential programs tied to local labor shortages. In Europe, the European Commission's Pact for Skills had attracted more than 3,200 organizations by late 2024, with members pledging large-scale upskilling and reskilling commitments (European Commission, accessed 2026-03-25). Singapore's SkillsFuture program continues to provide individual learning credits and mid-career support, including enhanced subsidies for workers aged 40 and above (SkillsFuture Singapore, accessed 2026-03-25). These programs suggest that effective reskilling is usually continuous, locally delivered, and linked to real vacancies rather than generic digital-skills messaging.

Social safety nets remain central during AI-driven transition periods because retraining alone does not solve income volatility. The OECD has warned that non-standard workers and lower-income households are less likely to be covered adequately by traditional unemployment and training systems (OECD Employment Outlook, 2024-07). The U.S. Congressional Budget Office has documented how unemployment insurance helps stabilize household income during job loss, even though coverage and benefit adequacy vary substantially across states (CBO, 2024-04-18). For households, practical preparation often includes maintaining an emergency buffer, using public workforce centers early rather than after long unemployment spells, and checking eligibility for wage insurance, training grants, childcare support, broadband subsidies, and health coverage. These recommendations are not financial advice but provide a framework for discussion.

Universal basic income and related cash-transfer experiments offer mixed but useful lessons. Results from the two-year randomized OpenResearch study found that unconditional cash recipients worked slightly fewer hours on average, while spending more time on caregiving, health, and job search flexibility (OpenResearch, accessed 2026-03-25). In Finland, the national basic income experiment found improved life satisfaction, mental wellbeing, and trust, but limited overall employment effects (Finnish Ministry of Social Affairs and Health, 2020-05-06). In Stockton, California, the SEED guaranteed income evaluation found recipients were more likely to obtain full-time employment and reported lower income volatility, anxiety, and depression (SEED, 2021-03). The clearest takeaway is not that one income model solves automation risk everywhere, but that predictable cash support can reduce crisis decision-making and make retraining, caregiving, and job search more manageable.

Community economic resilience is often strongest where local institutions spread risk across sectors and keep support close to residents. The Aspen Institute has documented how sector partnerships, worker-centered workforce boards, and employer collaboratives can improve placement and retention by aligning training with regional industry demand (Aspen Institute, accessed 2026-03-25). The Brookings Institution has argued that regional "good jobs" strategies work best when cities combine workforce development, small-business support, transportation access, and digital inclusion (Brookings, 2024-02-08). In practice, communities can build resilience through community colleges, public libraries, union training funds, apprenticeship intermediaries, cooperatives, and local entrepreneurship networks. For individuals, this means that local relationships, not just online courses, often determine who hears about opportunities first and who receives support during transition.

Corporate transition support is another major variable. The World Economic Forum reported that 50% of employers planned to accelerate automation in response to digital transformation pressures, while a large share also expected to invest in training workers for new roles (WEF, 2023-04-30). More recent employer guidance emphasizes skills-based workforce planning, internal mobility, and transparent redeployment pathways rather than abrupt layoffs. McKinsey has advised employers to map tasks, identify adjacent skills, and redesign jobs so workers can move into higher-value activities as AI adoption grows (McKinsey, 2023-07-26). Workers can respond by documenting transferable skills, seeking AI-adjacent responsibilities inside current roles, and asking employers about training budgets, credential reimbursement, and internal mobility policies before disruption becomes urgent.

Equitable access remains a core resilience issue because AI benefits are uneven when people lack devices, broadband, or foundational digital skills. The U.S. Federal Communications Commission reported that the Affordable Connectivity Program stopped accepting new applications in February 2024 because Congress did not provide additional funding (FCC, accessed 2026-03-25), increasing affordability risks for low-income households. At the same time, the National Telecommunications and Information Administration continues to administer the Digital Equity Act programs created by the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (NTIA, accessed 2026-03-25), which support device access, digital-skills training, and inclusion planning. Internationally, the International Telecommunication Union estimated that 67% of the world's population was using the internet in 2023, leaving roughly 2.6 billion people offline (ITU, 2024). If AI-enabled work, education, and public services expand faster than access does, existing inequality can harden into long-term exclusion.

A practical resilience strategy for households and communities usually includes five elements: building a financial buffer where possible, maintaining access to health care and mental health support, updating skills through trusted local institutions, strengthening professional and community networks, and securing reliable internet and device access. The Federal Reserve's latest household wellbeing survey found that many adults still struggle to cover unexpected expenses, underscoring how fragile many households remain before any technology shock occurs (Federal Reserve, 2024-05). That is why resilience planning should be framed as social infrastructure, not just personal responsibility. Communities that combine income stability, training access, trusted institutions, and digital inclusion are better positioned to absorb AI-driven change without deepening inequality.

AI Misinformation & Reality Distortion

The risks from AI-generated misinformation are now more concrete, measurable, and operationalized than they were even two years ago. In 2025, the World Economic Forum ranked misinformation and disinformation among the top short-term global risks (WEF, 2025), while the World Economic Forum's 2024 report had already identified misinformation and disinformation as the top global risk over the next two years (WEF, 2024). At the same time, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security warned that generative AI can facilitate the production of convincing but false content at scale (DHS, 2025). For individuals, this means the information environment is not merely noisier, it is increasingly engineered to exploit attention, trust, and emotion.

The erosion of shared reality

Deepfake capabilities have improved rapidly across video, audio, image, and identity simulation. The National Institute of Standards and Technology reported in 2024 that face-swap and face-generation methods continue to challenge forensic detection, especially when media are compressed or modified for online sharing (NIST, 2024). At the same time, the U.S. Department of Commerce noted that content provenance standards such as C2PA are being advanced to help users verify the origin and editing history of digital media (NIST, 2024). These detection and provenance tools are improving, but they remain unevenly adopted, which means ordinary users still often encounter synthetic content without reliable labels.

The 'liar's dividend' has become a practical civic problem, not just a theoretical one. The RAND Corporation's original work on the "firehose of falsehood" propaganda model (RAND, 2016) described how high-volume, rapid, repetitive, and multi-channel falsehoods can be persuasive even without credibility. That framework remains relevant because generative AI lowers the cost of producing exactly this type of content. The U.S. Office of the Director of National Intelligence assessed in 2024 that foreign actors are increasingly using generative AI to enhance influence operations, including by producing synthetic text, images, audio, and video (ODNI, 2024).

False and misleading information still spreads faster and farther when it is emotionally charged, novel, or identity-reinforcing. The often-cited MIT study found that false news reached more people and spread more rapidly than true news on Twitter (Science, 2018). More recent psychological work has strengthened the explanation: susceptibility often reflects a mix of inattention, motivated reasoning, and social identity, rather than simple ignorance. A 2024 review in Nature Reviews Psychology concluded that misinformation susceptibility is shaped by cognitive, social, and contextual factors, and that interventions such as prebunking can help, but effects are modest and context-dependent (Nature Reviews Psychology, 2024).

Mental health & social isolation

Repeated exposure still matters, even when people think they are being skeptical. Research on the illusory truth effect has repeatedly shown that repetition increases perceived accuracy of false claims (Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2015), and later work has found that the effect persists in digital environments where users encounter the same narratives across multiple formats and communities. The U.S. Surgeon General warned in 2024 that loneliness, social fragmentation, and weak trust can worsen vulnerability to harmful online experiences (HHS, 2024). For individuals under economic or emotional stress, synthetic media can intensify confusion, paranoia, and withdrawal, especially when friends, family, or local networks no longer agree on what is real.

Misinformation vulnerability is not evenly distributed, but it is also not limited to any one political or demographic group. A 2024 National Academies report found that susceptibility depends on exposure, digital literacy, trust, identity, and platform design, rather than a single fixed trait (National Academies, 2024). This matters for preparation: people who are tired, isolated, angry, or overloaded are often easier to manipulate. Practical defenses include slowing down before sharing, checking whether a claim appears in multiple independent outlets, and treating emotionally provocative audio or video as unverified until provenance or corroboration is available.

Weaponized information & democratic decay

Election-related synthetic media is no longer hypothetical. In January 2024, voters in New Hampshire received a robocall that used an AI-generated voice resembling President Joe Biden; the U.S. Federal Communications Commission later confirmed that AI-generated voices in robocalls are considered 'artificial' under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (FCC, 2024), and the New Hampshire Department of Justice announced criminal charges and civil enforcement actions tied to the spoofed AI robocall operation (New Hampshire DOJ, 2024). The case showed how cheap synthetic audio can be used to suppress turnout, impersonate trusted figures, and create confusion at critical moments.

Governments and election bodies are responding, but enforcement remains patchy. The European Union's AI Act requires certain AI-generated or manipulated content disclosures, including transparency duties for deepfakes in relevant contexts, as set out in the final legal text adopted in 2024, with phased implementation beginning in 2025-2026, including transparency obligations for synthetic content (EU, 2024). In the United States, the Federal Election Commission began considering rulemaking related to fraudulent misrepresentation involving AI in campaign communications (FEC, 2024), while several states enacted or expanded election-deepfake laws in 2024-2025, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures' tracking of state legislation on elections and deepfakes (NCSL, 2025). The regulatory trend is clear, but legal protections still vary widely by jurisdiction.

Personalized manipulation at scale

Platform and model-provider policies have become more explicit, but they do not eliminate risk. Meta states that it labels digitally created, altered, or AI-generated content when sufficient indicators are present (Meta, 2024). YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content (YouTube, 2024). OpenAI says it restricts deceptive political use and provides provenance-related tools for generated media in some contexts (OpenAI, 2025), and Google has described SynthID watermarking and disclosure approaches for AI-generated media (Google DeepMind, 2024). These steps improve transparency, but labels are inconsistently applied, can be stripped during reposting, and do not solve the problem of persuasive false framing around authentic media.

The deeper risk is not only fake content, but tailored influence operations that exploit personal data and behavioral signals. The National Security Agency, CISA, and FBI warned in 2024 that foreign influence actors adapt quickly to new technologies and combine synthetic content with account impersonation, audience targeting, and rapid amplification (CISA, NSA, FBI, 2024). This is the modern form of the firehose model: high volume, many channels, low regard for consistency, and continuous iteration based on audience response. For individuals, the most useful preparation is behavioral rather than technical, reduce impulsive sharing, diversify information sources, prioritize outlets that correct errors transparently, and assume that highly emotional political media may be optimized for reaction rather than truth.

A realistic preparation strategy focuses on verification habits, emotional self-regulation, and community trust. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advises users to verify the source, examine the full context, and rely on official channels for election information (CISA, 2024). In practice, that means pausing before reacting, checking whether audio or video has a clear provenance trail, and discussing suspicious content with trusted contacts before spreading it. In a distorted information environment, your strongest defenses are slower judgment, stronger local relationships, and a willingness to leave uncertain claims unshared.

Community & Support

Community resilience is strongest where people already have trusted local ties, accessible gathering spaces, and clear ways to help one another during disruption https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-regional-outlook-2023_4beaaa8c-en.html (OECD Regional Outlook, 2023, accessed 2026-03-25). Evidence from public health and disaster research shows that social connectedness improves recovery, reduces isolation, and supports mental wellbeing during periods of stress and uncertainty https://www.cdc.gov/social-connectedness/risk-factors/index.html (CDC, 2024, accessed 2026-03-25). For people navigating AI-driven labour disruption and information overload, the practical lesson is to strengthen local relationships before a crisis, not only after one begins.

Successful community models tend to share a few features. The World Health Organization identifies risk communication, community engagement, and trusted local participation as core components of resilient response systems https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-2019-nCoV-RCCE-2024.1 (WHO, 2024, accessed 2026-03-25). The National Academies has also found that social cohesion and cross-sector coordination, including links between residents, civil society, and local institutions, are central to community resilience https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25383/building-and-measuring-community-resilience-actions-for-communities-and-the (National Academies, 2019, accessed 2026-03-25). In practice, this means communities are better prepared when libraries, schools, faith groups, unions, neighbourhood associations, and local clinics already know how to share information and refer people to support.

Mental health access remains a major constraint, which makes community-based support especially important. The World Health Organization reported that around one in eight people globally were living with a mental disorder https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-disorders (WHO, 2022, accessed 2026-03-25), while the treatment gap remains large in many countries, especially for low-income and underserved groups https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/treatment-care/mental-health-gap-action-programme (WHO mhGAP, accessed 2026-03-25). In the United States, 988 provides nationwide call, text, and chat access to crisis support https://988lifeline.org/current-events/the-lifeline-and-988/ (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, accessed 2026-03-25), and SAMHSA highlights telehealth as a way to expand mental health and substance use care access https://www.samhsa.gov/behavioral-health-equity/telehealth (SAMHSA, accessed 2026-03-25). These tools do not replace in-person care, but they can lower barriers when job loss, caregiving burdens, transport limits, or stigma make traditional services hard to reach.

Peer-support networks work best when they are structured, bounded, and connected to professional care. Mental Health America describes peer support as emotional, informational, and practical help grounded in shared experience https://mhanational.org/position-statements/peer-support-services/ (Mental Health America, accessed 2026-03-25). The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends Psychological First Aid principles such as listening, practical assistance, connection to services, and support for coping https://www.cdc.gov/mentalhealth/stress-coping/psychological-first-aid/index.html (CDC, 2024, accessed 2026-03-25). A useful local framework is to train volunteers and community leaders to do four things consistently: notice distress, check in without judgment, share verified resources, and escalate to clinicians or crisis lines when someone may be at risk.

Communities also need collective defenses against manipulation and misinformation. Research from Sander van der Linden and colleagues shows that prebunking, exposing people to weakened examples of manipulation techniques before they encounter them in the wild, can improve resistance to misinformation https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abo2346 (Science Advances, 2022, accessed 2026-03-25). The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency recommends simple verification habits such as checking original sources, corroborating claims, and pausing before sharing emotionally charged content https://www.cisa.gov/topics/election-security/mis-disinformation (CISA, accessed 2026-03-25). Community groups can turn this into practice by running short workshops on deepfakes, reverse image search, source verification, and common manipulation tactics, especially before elections, protests, or local emergencies.

Local organizing is more effective when the goal is specific and the workflow is simple. The National Institute of Standards and Technology notes that trustworthy AI governance depends on accountability, transparency, and clear roles for people making and reviewing decisions https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework (NIST AI RMF, accessed 2026-03-25). At community level, that translates into practical toolkits: keep a shared list of vetted mental health, employment, and legal resources; assign a small moderation team for local online groups; create a rumor-check channel that requires source links; hold monthly digital literacy sessions; and document referral pathways for people facing harassment, fraud, or acute distress. UNESCO's media and information literacy work also emphasizes that critical evaluation and civic participation are teachable community skills, not just individual traits https://www.unesco.org/en/media-information-literacy (UNESCO, accessed 2026-03-25).

For individuals, the most effective preparation is usually local and relational. Join one trusted group that meets regularly, learn where crisis and counselling services are available, practice verification before sharing dramatic content, and identify two or three people you can contact if work disruption or online manipulation begins to affect your mental health. Strong social support is associated with better health and resilience outcomes https://www.cdc.gov/social-connectedness/about/index.html (CDC, 2024, accessed 2026-03-25). In an AI-driven society, community is not a soft extra, it is part of the core infrastructure people use to stay informed, grounded, and able to act.
